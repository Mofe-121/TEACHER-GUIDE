# TEACHER-GUIDE
Guide for designing an AI-IBED-aligned instruction design

This guide shows you how to plan a science lesson that is built around inquiry, tied to standards, and supported by AI in a responsible way. It follows Understanding by Design (backward design): you start with the big idea and the assessment evidence, and only then plan the 5E learning sequence (Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, Evaluate).

The guide walks through a finished Grade 6 lesson on air pressure. The lesson is aligned to Virginia SOLs 6.1 and 6.7, NGSS crosscutting concepts, and INTASC standards. Each row of the template comes with:

## What it means: the purpose of each row in the lesson template
__Look out for:__ common mistakes
__Weak and strong examples:__ from the sample lesson
__Standards check:__ so, the lesson meets the level of the standard it claims
Transferable method: so, you can apply the approach to any state's standards

__AI use:__ prompts for tools like Claude, ChatGPT, and NotebookLM, with notes on where AI helps and where it gets things wrong.
The guide ends with an activity sheet, a rubric, and three self-audits covering standards alignment, inquiry integrity, and AI integration.

## Who should use this guide?

__Pre-service teachers:__ learn backward design and 5E planning, and prepare lesson plans that hold up in methods courses, field placements, and licensure reviews.
__In-service teachers:__ tighten existing lessons, check that they really align to standards, and add AI to your planning without losing your own judgment as a professional.
__Teacher educators:__ use it as a model and a teaching tool in methods courses, as a shared language for feedback, and as a starting point for talking about using AI responsibly in lesson design.

# Engineering Design Challenge Template: Electricity (Night Light)
This template is built on the Lesson Plan Template and Teacher Guide. The lesson plan uses inquiry to help students work out why circuits behave as they do. This template is the next step: students use what they found to design and build a solution to a real problem. Inquiry asks, "why does it work this way?" Design asks "now build something with it."

## The challenge
Student teams design a night light for the fictional Global Illumination Festival, an event celebrating unity, diversity, and innovation. Each night light must:
Represent a cultural or historical event (one they make up, one from the U.S., or one from another country)
Use at least three bulbs and at least one switch
Use a series circuit (Good), a parallel circuit (Great), or both combined (Excellent)
Look good and come with a schematic diagram showing which way the current flows

## What's included
Student handout organized around the engineering design cycle: Ask → Imagine → Plan → Create → Improve → Explaining the Results → Posing Other Solutions, with space for brainstorming, sketches, circuit diagrams, and reflection questions
Three-level rubric (Good / Great / Excellent) scoring the event connection, circuit design, and look of the night light, with its explanation and diagram
Teacher guide extension that explains how the challenge extends the inquiry lesson's evaluation phase. For each phase it gives:
What it means: the goal of that phase
Teacher moves: specific ways to guide students
Weak vs. strong examples: what shallow and rigorous work look like in practice

## Design principles
__Evidence over new facts.__ Every phase sends students back to the data they collected in the inquiry process.
The diagram is the deliverable. Materials stay locked until the circuit plan shows components, connection points, and current direction.
Testing is its own step. Each criterion is tested separately, and students predict the result before they close the switch.
Revision needs a reason. Every change has to name the failure and its cause, then be tested again.

## How to use it
Teach the inquiry lesson from the Lesson Plan Template first. Then run this challenge as the extension or evaluation activity. You can swap the festival theme, the bulb and switch minimums, or the rubric levels to fit your grade level and the materials you have.
